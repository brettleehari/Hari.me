Nothing left this device.

Most of us use AI through a website. You type, something happens somewhere, an answer comes back. It feels like a place you send a request to.

The next twenty minutes take that apart.

The thing in your pocket is doing the same arithmetic as the rack in the data centre. It just has a smaller budget.

It is a file. That is the whole surprise.

what is running

gemma-3-4b-it-Q4_K_M.gguf

2.49 GB. One file. Copy it to a pen drive and it runs on your machine offline, forever. It does not expire or check in with anyone.

what is inside it

3.88 billion numbers

Nobody wrote them. They were fitted, by machine, against an enormous amount of text. Nobody can point at any one of them and say what it does.

Nobody designed the footpath across the park. Thousands walked the shortest route, the grass wore down, and now there is a path. It is real, it works, and there is no document anywhere specifying it.

It guesses the next word. That is it.

Given everything so far, what comes next. Ranked list, one gets picked, it is added to the text, and the whole thing runs again from the top. Guess a word. Add it. Guess again.

Type "I am running" and your phone suggests "late". Same mechanism. Yours read your messages; this one read more text than a person could get through in a thousand lifetimes. Same trick, absurd scale.

It predicts. It does not retrieve.

Which is why it makes things up. There is no fact database to check against. There is nothing to look up. There is only the guess.

Store the numbers roughly. It still works.

Every weight started at high precision — that file is about 8 GB. Round them off, fewer digits each, and the file quarters. The model is still recognisably itself.

Every photo on your phone is a JPEG. Every song you streamed is compressed. A WhatsApp voice note is nothing like studio audio and nobody has ever complained, because it fits, it sends, and it is good enough.

3.88 B × 0.6 = 2.49 GB

Roughly 0.6 GB per billion parameters, at Q4. That is the whole arithmetic.

Every word costs one full read of the file.

To produce one word, the machine reads all 2.49 GB out of memory. Not part of it. There is no relevant section. Then it does the same thing again for the next word.

So speed is not really about how clever the chip is — the arithmetic is trivial and the chip spends most of its time waiting. It is about how fast the device pulls bytes out of memory, divided by how big the file is.

how fast it readshow big the file is = words per second
Want it faster? Faster memory, or a smaller file. That is the entire optimisation space.

72 ÷ 2.49 = 29. I measured 22.3.

the phone

theoretical ceiling

Bandwidth divided by file size. Nothing else.

actually measured

77% of ceiling. 325 ms to first token.

Real hardware lands at 50–80% of the ceiling. Kernel overhead, attention over the cache, sampling — all of it costs something. 77% is a good result, not a discrepancy.

A long, sustained generation settled at 20.33 tok/s — about 9% off the cold number. That is heat.

The same division works on a $30,000 GPU.

The data-centre card is not doing something different. It reads the whole file for every word, exactly like the phone. It just reads about fifty times faster.

PART TWO

One file.
Four places.

Same model, same quantization, same engine — an iPhone, a MacBook, a rented GPU, and Amazon Bedrock. We measure all four and find where the division holds.

Open weights is not open source.

what you get

Open weights

The trained parameters, downloadable. Gemma, Qwen, Llama, DeepSeek, gpt-oss. Runs anywhere, offline, no API call.

what you usually don't

Open source

Training data, training code, and a license with no use restrictions. Very few models clear that bar.

what changes your risk

The license

Apache 2.0 and MIT are permissive. Gemma ships under Google's own terms with an acceptable-use policy. Read it before you ship.

The licenses do not line up with the flags.

Qwen is Apache 2.0. DeepSeek is MIT. Gemma carries usage restrictions Google can update. On the axis enterprises actually get burned by, country of origin tells you very little — and for local inference, airplane mode is the guarantee anyway.

Hybrid attention: 17.8 GB of cache down to 3.2.

Five local layers for every one global layer. Local layers attend to a fixed 1,024-token window no matter how long the conversation gets. Only the global layers see everything.

Cache per token per layer: 2 × 4 heads × 256 dim × 2 bytes = 4 KB.

On an H100 the equation stops binding.

iPhone

77% of ceiling

2.49 GB against 72 GB/s. The memory bus is saturated. Bandwidth is genuinely the constraint.

H100

~25% of ceiling

The model is too small to keep a 3350 GB/s bus busy. Kernel launch, sampling and scheduling overhead dominate instead.

The equation did not fail. The bottleneck moved.

A formula that always works teaches you to trust formulas. A formula with a visible edge teaches you to ask which constraint binds. That is the part worth taking home.

Batching breaks the constraint.

Read the model once, use it for many users at the same time. The 2.49 GB read gets amortised across the whole batch. This is the answer to "if it's memory-bound, why does anyone buy an H100."

concurrent streamsper usertotal throughput$ / 1M tokens
1~210 tok/s210high

Per user falls. Total climbs about eight-fold. This is why hosted APIs are cheap.

Prefill is compute-bound. Decode is memory-bound.

prefill · reading your prompt

325 ms on the phone

The whole prompt processes in parallel, saturating the arithmetic units. TFLOPs matter here. This is the wait before the first word.

decode · writing the answer

45 ms per token

One token at a time, whole model read each time. Bandwidth decides. For anything generating prose, this is nearly all the wall clock.

If your workload is enormous prompts and tiny outputs — classification, extraction, reranking — the ranking of hardware reverses, and a compute-heavy card with modest bandwidth becomes the right buy.

what you gain

Sixty seconds

No quota, no instance, no SSH, no key pair, nothing to terminate. 128K context. Thirteen regions.

what you lose

The x-axis

No bandwidth figure, no known precision, no batch size. This tier cannot go on the chart — and that absence is the point.

One user cannot fill a GPU.

At low volume a hosted API is cheaper than a GPU you rent by the hour. Self-hosting wins on data residency, latency, no rate limits, and models nobody offers. Price is not on that list until the machine runs hot.

The reason to learn tiers one to three is to understand what tier four is doing on your behalf — and to know when it stops being the cheaper answer.

PART THREE

Now put it
in your pocket.

No account, no card, no cloud. Everyone in this room can do this at the same time, on the phone already in their hand.

Four steps.

  • Install PocketPal AI from the App Store. Free, open source, runs GGUF files through llama.cpp — the same engine used on every tier tonight.
  • Download Gemma 3 1B, Q4_K_M — about 800 MB. Models tab → search Hugging Face. Start this first; the wifi is the slow part, not the phone.
  • Check Metal is on in the model settings. Without GPU acceleration you get a CPU number and it looks four times slower than it should.
  • Airplane mode, then ask it something. PocketPal reports tokens/sec under each response.

Predict your own number before you read it. Your phone's bandwidth, divided by 0.8 GB, times roughly 0.7. An iPhone 15 and an iPhone 17 in this room will disagree — and the equation explains the gap.

Airplane mode, together.

Radios off. Ask it something at the same time. Fifty phones, no network, all generating.

It is a stunt, and it is also the honest summary. These things are files. They run on hardware you own. The speed is a division you can do in your head.
